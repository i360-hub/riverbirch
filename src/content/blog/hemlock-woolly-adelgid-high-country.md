If your hemlocks are looking thin, gray, and tired, there is a good chance one small insect is behind it. Here is how to check your own trees in five minutes, what your real options are, and the honest line between a tree that can still be treated and one that needs to come down.

> Turn a low hemlock branch over and look where the needles meet the twig. Small white cottony tufts lined up along the underside are hemlock woolly adelgid. It feeds on the tree's stored starches, and untreated it kills eastern and Carolina hemlocks in roughly 4 to 10 years. Late fall is the easiest time of year to spot it — the wool is on the twigs right now.

## How to spot it on your own trees

You do not need any equipment for this. Pick a low branch, turn it over, and look at the base of the needles along the twig. The adelgid itself is tiny and dark and easy to miss. Its wool is not: small round white tufts, like bits of cotton, sitting in a row where the needles attach. NC Cooperative Extension describes the same sign — white, woolly egg sacs on the undersides of hemlock branches — and notes that early detection is possible precisely because the wool shows.

Then step back and read the whole tree:

- **Needles gone dull or gray-green** instead of glossy dark green
- **Little or no new growth** at the branch tips in spring
- **Needles dropping** and the crown thinning, so you can see sky through it
- **Branches dying back** from the bottom and the inside of the crown outward

One or two of those without wool can mean drought or root stress. Wool on the twigs plus a thinning crown is the adelgid, and on the properties we visit it is now the usual story for a declining hemlock.

## What it does to the tree

The insect settles at the base of the needles and feeds on the starches the tree stores in its twigs — the reserves a hemlock needs to leaf out and grow. Starved of those reserves year after year, the tree stops making new growth, sheds needles, and loses branches. Eastern and Carolina hemlocks have no real defense against it. Left untreated, most infested hemlocks die within about 4 to 10 years.

The last part of that decline is the part that matters for your property: a dead hemlock dries out and turns brittle, and brittle wood does not behave predictably when it fails — or when a crew has to take it apart over a roof.

## Treat it or remove it?

**Treatment is real, and it is not our job.** We will say that plainly: River Birch Tree Service does not treat hemlock woolly adelgid. Treatment means pesticides, and in North Carolina applying pesticides for hire takes a license. Any company that offers to treat your hemlocks for pay should be able to show you that license. A homeowner can treat their own trees without a license. A company doing it for pay needs one.

If your tree still has a good share of live crown, treatment is worth pricing before you talk about removal at all. Start here:

- **NC Cooperative Extension, Watauga County Center** — local, current treatment guidance for homeowners
- **NC Forest Service** — identification help and forest-health advice
- **Hemlock Restoration Initiative** — the western NC program built specifically around saving hemlocks; their site walks through treatment options in plain language
- **A licensed applicator** — for the treatment itself

**Removal is our lane, and here is when it is the right call.** Around [Deep Gap](/tree-service-deep-gap-nc), a good share of the hazard removals we get called for are hemlocks the adelgid has already killed — trees that stood in the woods for decades become a problem the year they die over a driveway, a road, or a roofline. A declining hemlock is a removal candidate when:

| What you see | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Wool on twigs, crown still full | Early infestation | Get a treatment opinion now — this is the stage Extension says treatment works best |
| Crown thinning, little new growth | Mid-decline | Treatment decision is time-sensitive; do not wait another year |
| More dead than alive in the crown | Late decline | Ask about treatment, but price the removal too |
| Crown mostly dead, tree brittle, near a structure | Hazard tree | Remove it — it only gets more dangerous and more expensive |

Dead hemlocks cost more to remove than live ones, for the same reason dead trees always do: the wood is unpredictable, so the work goes slower and more carefully. Waiting does not save money on a tree that is already gone. November is the right time to look. The woolly masses are on the twigs and easy to see, a treated tree gets help before another growing season of feeding, and a dead one can come down on a scheduled visit instead of an emergency call after winter weather loads it up.
