If you live above 3,000 feet in the High Country, ice storms aren't a matter of if — they're a matter of when. A quarter inch of ice adds hundreds of pounds to a canopy, and our steep lots and tall white pines take the worst of it. After every big ice event, my phone lights up. Here's what I tell every caller, in the order that matters.

> In the first 48 hours after an ice storm: stay away from hanging limbs and leaning trees, photograph everything before any cleanup for your insurance claim, and call a pro for anything on a structure, blocking access, or near a power line. Don't cut limbs under tension yourself — that's how people get hurt.

## First: don't touch anything dangerous

Ice-loaded limbs snap without warning, and a partially fallen tree is a loaded spring. The two things that hurt homeowners after storms are hanging limbs ("widowmakers") and trunks bent under tension. If a limb is caught up in the canopy above you, or a trunk is leaning hard against another tree, stay out from under it. No tree is worth an injury — that's what crews with the right rigging are for.

If a tree or limb is on a power line, that's the power company's job, not a tree service's. Call Blue Ridge Energy (or your provider) and keep everyone clear until they say otherwise.

## Second: document everything before cleanup

Before anyone — you, me, or a neighbor with a chainsaw — touches anything, walk the property and photograph it all. Wide shots showing where the tree was, close-ups of the damage to structures, the broken limbs, the root plate if it uprooted. Your insurance adjuster wasn't here for the storm; your photos are the claim. This five-minute step is the difference between a smooth claim and a fight.

## Third: triage — what needs a crew now vs. what can wait

**Call now** for: a tree on your house, garage, or deck; anything blocking your driveway or the road; limbs tangled with service lines to your house; a tree leaning hard toward a structure that could go in the next wind.

**Can wait a few days** for: limbs down in the yard away from structures; cosmetic canopy damage on otherwise healthy trees; brush piles that aren't blocking anything.

We prioritize [storm and ice damage calls](/storm-damage-tree-removal) during operating hours — the scene gets made safe first, then the tree comes off the structure in sections, then debris gets hauled. Monday through Saturday, 7 AM to 7 PM.

## Can the tree be saved?

Not every ice-damaged tree is a removal. Here's my honest triage:

- **Probably savable:** clean limb breaks on a healthy tree, less than a third of the canopy lost. Proper pruning cuts at the branch collar, and the tree compartmentalizes the wound.
- **Maybe:** bark torn but the trunk intact, up to half the canopy gone. Worth a professional look — species and structure decide it.
- **Likely a removal:** split trunk, bark stripped down one side, more than half the canopy gone, root plate lifted, or a lean that wasn't there before the storm.

White pines — our most common storm casualty — are brittle-topped and don't recover well from major crown loss. Oaks handle it better. Hemlocks already stressed by woolly adelgid often don't bounce back. I'd rather tell you a tree can be saved with [pruning](/tree-trimming-pruning) than sell you a removal — a quick look is all it takes, and estimates are free.

## What about insurance?

The short version: insurance usually covers the *damage the tree caused* — to your house, garage, fence, deck — minus your deductible. Most policies also include some debris removal coverage, often capped around $500–$1,000 total. What they generally won't cover is removing a tree that fell in the yard without damaging anything — that's on the homeowner. Every policy is different, so read yours, but those photos you took in step two are what make the claim work either way.

## Preventing the next one

You can't ice-proof a forest, but you can tilt the odds:

- **Deadwood pruning** before winter removes the limbs most likely to fail under ice load.
- **Crown thinning** on dense pines lets wind and ice shed instead of accumulate.
- **Remove the obvious hazards** — the dead ash, the leaning pine over the driveway — before storm season, not after.
- **Plant smart:** native species suited to your elevation and exposure handle High Country weather better than ornamentals chosen for looks.

Late fall is the right time for this work, before the ice arrives. One visit can take the worst risks off the board.
